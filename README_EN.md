# Insurance Consumption

An empirical analysis of insurance consumption among families with students enrolled at the Center for Applied Social Sciences (CCSA), 
from an Actuarial Science perspective.

Based on data collected through a questionnaire, this study describes the sample profile and compares different groups to investigate 
differences in insurance consumption.

## Full Academic Work

The complete undergraduate thesis is available through the Institutional Repository of the Federal University of Paraíba (UFPB).

**Original title:** *Consumo de seguros das famílias que possuem estudantes no Centro de Ciências Sociais Aplicadas em sua composição: uma análise empírica sob a ótica da Ciência Atuarial.*

## Methodology

Applied, quantitative, descriptive, and exploratory research based on data collected through a structured questionnaire. The study used 
a non-probability convenience sample of 135 respondents affiliated with CCSA/UFPB. The database was cleaned and standardized, followed 
by descriptive and comparative statistical analyses using R.

## Analyses Performed

1. Sample characterization: demographic, socioeconomic, and financial profile of CCSA/UFPB students, with particular emphasis on Actuarial Science students due to their specific academic background in risk and insurance.
2. Socioeconomic and educational profile: distribution by degree program, educational attainment of the head of household, household composition, and family income.
3. Financial knowledge and risk perception.
4. Insurance consumption and household decision-making, determinants of insurance purchase, types of insurance, previous insurance experience, and comparison between groups.

## Key Findings

Approximately 55% of the families reported having some type of insurance. Higher insurance consumption was observed among higher-income groups. Price, coverage, and payment conditions were among the most relevant factors influencing insurance purchase. Decision-making tended to be individual rather than necessarily shared within the household. No statistically significant difference in insurance consumption was identified between Actuarial Science students and students from the other degree programs analyzed.

## Limitations

The study used a non-probability convenience sample restricted to CCSA/UFPB, with a reduced number of valid observations in specific analyses after data filtering. Given the research design, the results do not support causal inference.

## Future Research

- Apply multivariate models, such as logistic regression, to estimate the probability that a family holds insurance based on different respondent characteristics.
- Jointly investigate family income, financial knowledge, and academic background, including potential interaction effects on insurance consumption.
- Extend data collection to other degree programs, academic centers, and educational institutions to increase sample heterogeneity and representativeness.
- Incorporate behavioral economics variables such as loss aversion, perceived probability, overconfidence, present bias, trust in insurers, and perceived cost-benefit of insurance.
- Investigate purchase intention by distinguishing families that currently have no insurance but show a propensity to purchase from those that effectively reject insurance products.
- Model the probability of insurance purchase separately by line of business to investigate whether the determinants of life and personal insurance differ from those associated with property and casualty insurance.
- Develop insurance propensity models to identify which characteristics provide the greatest explanatory and predictive value for insurance purchase.

## Conclusions

The results did not provide evidence of a statistically significant difference in insurance consumption between families with Actuarial Science students and those associated with the other degree programs analyzed. Therefore, an actuarial academic background, when considered in isolation, was not sufficient to differentiate insurance consumption behavior within the sample.

The findings suggest that insurance purchase decisions may be better understood through the interaction of socioeconomic conditions, financial knowledge, risk perception, and behavioral factors, with family income showing a relevant relationship with the observed insurance consumption pattern.

Future research may extend this perspective by incorporating additional variables and statistical and predictive modeling techniques. Multivariate models could be used to investigate which characteristics have greater explanatory and predictive power regarding the probability of insurance purchase, demand for different types of coverage, and distinct consumer profiles.

In future applications using larger and more representative insurance-market datasets, this approach could be adapted and validated to identify policyholder profiles and behavioral patterns. In this context, Open Insurance represents a relevant avenue for future research by enabling, subject to consent and applicable data-protection requirements, standardized data sharing across the insurance ecosystem. Combining different data sources and dimensions could support research on purchase propensity, consumer segmentation, product and coverage preferences, and other patterns associated with insurance consumption decisions.

Greater data availability, however, does not automatically imply greater predictive performance. Any predictive gain should be assessed empirically through model development, comparison, and validation, identifying which variables effectively add explanatory and predictive value relative to baseline models and whether model performance generalizes to new samples.

Overall, this study provides an initial empirical foundation for a research agenda connecting Actuarial Science, consumer behavior, and predictive analytics in the insurance market, moving from the identification of differences between groups toward the investigation of factors that may help explain and predict insurance consumption behavior.
